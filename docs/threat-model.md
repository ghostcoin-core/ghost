# Threat model

Status: first version, written before the lifecycle specification. It covers the design in the [outline](ghost-outline.md) and the [base client decision](decisions/0001-base-client.md). It must be updated whenever the outline changes a claim, and checked again against the lifecycle specification when that is written. Updated 2026-10-20 for quantum-safe accounts from genesis and the one security target of [specification 09](spec/09-quantum-safe-accounts.md), and 2026-10-21 for the per-commitment salt (C20). Updated 2026-10-30 for rented and redirected hashrate: A4 and T4 now say a majority can be rented cheaply at launch, and section 9 gives the cost estimate, the confirmation depth by amount and the reorg guard decision.

The main network is labelled experimental at launch. All review so far is done with AI models, with no outside audit and no paid review. This document says what we think the design protects, what it does not, and what each protection depends on. It is a statement of intent to be tested, not a proof.

## How to read this document

- **Attackers** (T1 to T13) are the parties we design against, with what each can do.
- **Assumptions** (A1 to A12) are the things that must be true for a protection to hold. If one fails, every claim that lists it fails with it.
- **Claims** (C1 to C48) are the privacy and security claims the outline makes, each mapped to the attackers it defends against and the assumptions it rests on.
- Section 6 lists claims that cannot be defended as the outline words them today, with the wording we can defend.
- Section 9 estimates what a majority of mining power costs to rent at launch, gives the confirmation depth a recipient should wait for by amount, and records the decision on a reorg guard.
- [Privacy under a hostile, low-volume network](research/privacy-under-attack.md) works through amount matching, timing, spam cover, hostile endpoints and network observers with numbers, and separates settlement depth from privacy cover.

## 1. What is protected

Five properties are the core. Everything else in the claims table is a part of one of them.

1. **No counterfeit coins.** Coin value in the hidden set can only come from burned public GHOST, from authorised issuance, or from a value-conserving reveal of an existing coin commitment. A data commitment can never create money.
2. **No double reveal.** A commitment can be used up once. A second one-time reveal of the same commitment is rejected on the canonical chain. This is settled probabilistically (see A4).
3. **No link between a commitment and its reveal.** The reveal does not publish the commitment it spends, and no value appears in both the commit and the reveal transactions apart from a shared, widely used tree state. The linkage test checks this for coins and for data.
4. **Holder-only control.** Whoever holds the digits (the Phantom Key) controls the commitment and its remainder. Nobody without the digits can reveal it, take it, or stop it from being valid. No administrator, committee, contract owner or time-lock exists that could.
5. **Each proof is bound to its purpose.** A reveal proof commits to the recipient, the revealed amount, the remainder commitment, the one-time marker, the rules, the fee, the chain, the protocol version and a domain tag. A proof of holding is bound to the verifier, the chain and a challenge the verifier chooses. A copied proof cannot be used for anything else.

## 2. What is not protected

Stated plainly, so nobody relies on it by mistake.

- **Destinations, amounts, timing and network data can correlate.** A commit shows its sender and the amount burned. A reveal shows its recipient and any public amount. A commit and a reveal of the same unusual amount, close in time, or sent from the same network address, can be matched by anyone watching. The design removes the cryptographic link, not the statistical one.
- **A small crowd is a weak shield.** Privacy depends on how many unrevealed commitments look alike. Early in the chain's life that number is small.
- **Accounts are as safe as their signature scheme, and no safer than the contracts they trust.** From genesis, ordinary accounts sign with ML-DSA-44 or SLH-DSA and every secp256k1 transaction type is refused (specification 09). A contract that implements elliptic-curve cryptography in its own bytecode, or RSA through modexp, is only as safe as that cryptography, which a quantum attacker breaks; that is its author's risk. Addresses stay 20 bytes, so an address two parties agree on before it is funded (some CREATE2 setups) has only about 2^80 classical and 2^53 quantum collision resistance.
- **A bearer key has no recovery.** Lose the digits and the value or data is gone. Show the digits to someone and they hold full control; the first to reveal wins. There is no administrator to appeal to.
- **The chain can prove a commitment has not been revealed (as of a block). It can never prove nobody has viewed its contents.** The author, and anyone the digits are shown to, can read them.
- **Data that has been shown can be copied.** The key controls authority and access, not the bytes after they have been seen. The chain does not keep the data and does not promise the file stays available.
- **Removed from supply is not gone from existence.** When coins are committed they leave the public supply and become claims. Public accounting still tracks value entering and leaving the hidden set. The claim is "removed from supply", not "destroyed".
- **Settlement is probabilistic.** A reveal can be undone by a reorganisation. Waiting longer lowers the chance, and never makes it zero. Against an attacker who rents hashrate, waiting only raises the cost of the work it must do, and on a young chain that cost is small (section 9).
- **Software can be wrong.** The custom parts are new and have had only model-based review. A bug in them is not covered by any claim here.
- **Availability is not promised.** A miner or majority can delay or censor transactions. The chain can stall if most mining power leaves.

## 3. Attackers and their powers

| ID | Attacker | What it can do | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | **Chain observer** | Reads every block, header, transaction, state value, marker and encrypted note, forever. Builds indexes. Joins chain data with outside data such as an exchange account that funded a commit. | See inside a commitment, tell which commitment a marker belongs to, or read a note without the digits. |
| T2 | **Network observer** | Sees which network address sent or fetched what, and when: an internet provider, a node or endpoint operator, many hostile peers, a hosting provider. Can first-see a transaction at its entry point. | Read proofs' hidden inputs, or forge a transaction. Learns nothing about the digits from the traffic. |
| T3 | **Malicious miner** (less than half of mining power) | Chooses which transactions to include and in what order, delays or omits a reveal, sees the pending pool, bends timestamps within the allowed range, withholds blocks (selfish mining), mines a short private fork. Sees the first sender address of anything sent directly to it. | Mint outside the rules, include an invalid proof (other nodes reject the block), redirect a proof to another recipient, or reveal a commitment without the digits. |
| T4 | **Majority miner** (more than half) | Everything T3 can do, plus rewrite recent history, censor any transaction indefinitely, and reverse settlement. Can make a reveal vanish so the same commitment can be revealed again elsewhere (a double spend against a recipient). Can pick the heaviest chain at will. On a young RandomX chain this is not only a large mining operation: anyone who rents or redirects RandomX hashrate for an hour or so can be it, at an estimated cost of about a dollar an hour at the expected launch hashrate (section 9). | Create a valid proof for a statement that is false, mint outside the rules (full nodes still reject it), reveal a commitment it holds no digits for, or change rules on nodes that do not upgrade. |
| T5 | **Malicious web host or endpoint** | Serves altered app code that steals digits or shows a wrong amount. Runs a node endpoint that lies about the chain, shows stale or forked tree states, withholds history, or logs which commitments and witnesses a wallet asks for. | Break the proof system, or alter what is on the canonical chain. |
| T6 | **Anyone shown a Phantom Key** | Reveals first, sweeps the remainder, reads the encrypted note, derives the data key and decrypts the off-chain data, copies shown data. Includes the sender who keeps a copy, a backup service, someone who photographs the screen. | Be stopped by the chain once they hold the digits. |
| T7 | **Future quantum attacker** | Forges elliptic-curve and RSA signatures wherever they remain: web certificates, and contracts that implement such checks in their own bytecode. Ghost's accounts, node identities and node handshake use none (specification 09). Runs Grover-style search at roughly square-root cost against hashes and keys, and a quantum collision search with some further saving. May mine faster if quantum speedups apply to the work function. | Break a well-chosen hash by more than the square-root effect, or the zero-knowledge and soundness of a hash-based proof in the quantum random oracle model (see A1 and A2). |
| T8 | **Hostile peer** | Floods headers with valid structure and bad seals, sends malformed or maximum-size proofs and Reveal transactions, fills pools, squats markers with invalid reveals, withholds history, runs many nodes to eclipse a victim (Sybil). | Cause an invalid block to be accepted, if the node limits and checks hold. |
| T9 | **Private-fork attacker** | Mines a secret chain with a valid proof of work and shows it to a victim or to an off-chain verifier. Cost is mining power, not proof forgery. Combined with T8 it can eclipse a verifier who sees only headers. | Make a private fork heavier than the honest chain without more work. |
| T10 | **Malicious dependency, build or release channel** | Ships altered code through a compromised library, build tool, release signer, mirror, installer host or app store. | Change the chain's rules by software alone: nodes enforce them, and a bad release only affects those who run it. |
| T11 | **Commitment spammer** | Creates many commitments to fill the tree, bloat state, and shrink other users' anonymity set (the attacker knows which commitments are theirs, so the crowd really is smaller). Floods dust amounts. | Spend anyone else's commitment. |
| T12 | **Malicious verifier** (proof of holding) | Collects proofs, relays a challenge between a prover and another verifier, records which commitments a prover can show, tries to reuse a proof elsewhere. | Reuse a proof for another verifier, chain or challenge, or turn a proof of holding into a reveal. |

| T13 | **Malicious delegate or statement author** | Holds a delegated, scoped capability, or writes a developer-defined statement for the general proof checker. Tries to act beyond scope, budget or expiry, to pass off a delegated proof as a coin reveal, or to exhaust verification resources. | Use the general proof checker in place of the coin's checks, or act without a valid proof. |

The founder and maintainers are not a separate attacker class on the chain: they have no power over it (outline, Maintenance). They are covered by T10, because they can publish software.

## 4. Assumptions

| ID | Assumption | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | **Hash security.** The hash used inside proofs and trees, and the standard hash used for derivation, resist collision, preimage and second-preimage attacks at the stated level, with every use separately tagged. Standard authenticated encryption is secure. Against a quantum attacker, preimage search costs about the square root of the classical effort, so levels are stated after that reduction. | Fake membership, links or forged commitments become possible. |
| A2 | **Proof-system soundness.** The hash-based proof system is sound at a stated security level, including in the quantum random oracle model, and the library implementing it is correct. Soundness comes before browser speed in the choice. | A false statement can be proved: counterfeit coins, theft. |
| A3 | **Zero knowledge.** The proof system's privacy mode is on in the shipped configuration, and proof size, shape and timing leak nothing about the hidden inputs. | A proof can reveal which commitment it spends. |
| A4 | **Honest-majority hashrate, probabilistic settlement.** Honest miners hold the majority of mining power for as long as a payment needs to stay settled, and a recipient treats a reveal or transfer as final only after the depth section 9 gives for its amount. **Not expected to hold at launch for anyone who pays to break it.** RandomX hashrate can be rented (NiceHash) or redirected from Monero, as the Qubic pool did against Monero in August 2025, and Ghost starts thousands of times smaller than Monero: a majority of the expected launch hashrate is estimated to rent for about a dollar an hour (section 9). What the assumption can honestly rest on is cost: to undo a payment with `z` blocks on top, an attacker must do at least the work the honest network did on the payment's block and those `z` blocks, and it can do that privately during the wait, from the payment's parent, with barely more than the network's hashrate. Depth protects a payment only up to the rental cost of that work, less the block rewards the attacker keeps; once those rewards are worth as much as the rental, it protects nothing. The work function is assumed to give no large quantum advantage to one party, and RandomX is designed so that ordinary CPUs are the efficient hardware; whether dedicated RandomX machines now sold for Monero change that is not established here. | Reorganisations and double reveals become cheap. Below the depth in section 9 they already are cheap at launch. |
| A5 | **Correct custom code.** The commit and reveal statements, the coin accounting and origin rule, the private transaction and fee formula, key derivation from the digits, header encoding and the RandomX integration do what the specification says. The review behind this is model-based, with different models writing and reviewing, adversarial tests and written properties. There is no outside audit. | Any claim can fail in ways nothing else here covers. |
| A6 | **Secure randomness.** Every Phantom Key, and every commitment's salt, is generated by the app from a secure random source on an uncompromised device. | Keys can be guessed. A repeated or predictable salt brings back the guess-once-check-many shortcut for the leaves that share it (C20). |
| A7 | **Holder custody.** The holder keeps the digits secret, shows them only to someone they would trust with full control, and the device and screen they use are not compromised. | Bearer control is lost. |
| A8 | **Honest build and distribution.** The software a user runs is the published code: pinned and licence-checked dependencies, signed and rebuildable releases, mirrors, and a way for the user to check them out of band. | Keys and funds leak through the app or the node. |
| A9 | **History and connectivity.** At least one honest node keeps and serves commitment and note history and the block receipts that authenticate notes and spent markers (specification 05, sections 8 and 9), and each verifier or wallet can reach at least one honest peer and is not eclipsed. | Old keys cannot be restored, and a verifier can be shown a false chain. |
| A10 | **Crowd and behaviour.** Enough similar unrevealed commitments exist, and users do not tie their own transactions together by repeating amounts, timing or network address. | Privacy is nominal. |
| A11 | **Verifier identity.** For off-chain proofs of holding, the prover knows who the verifier is, and the verifier checks a recent header chain and the marker status itself. | A relayed or stale proof is accepted. |
| A12 | **Signature and key-exchange security.** ML-DSA-44 (FIPS 204) is unforgeable at NIST Category 2, resting on module lattices; SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) rests on hash security alone (A1); ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) is secure at Category 3, and the node handshake stays secret while either it or X25519 holds. Each implementation is correct and checks exact lengths and scheme IDs (specification 09). | Public balances can be spent by a forger, or node sessions read and impersonated. An account under the other scheme is unaffected. |
| A13 | **Pinned start.** The web app's header chain starts from the header its released build pins for the network: genesis, or a pinned header with its total work and the key blocks below it ([webapp](webapp.md#headers)). Every header after it is checked in the page, its difficulty and RandomX seal included, and the app takes its heights, coin roots and confirmations from the chain with the most work. The start header is the one thing the page takes on trust, through the build (A8). The released table is empty today, so the app shows no confirmations on any network until a launch network and its start are added. | A wrong pin makes the page follow a chain that is not the network's, and confirm what that chain shows. |

## 6. Claims that cannot be defended as written

These are places where the outline's wording promises more than this model supports. Each gets the wording we can defend. The outline is not changed by this document; a follow-up edit should adopt the wording.

1. **"Quantum-safe throughout" (C2).** Specification 09 makes ordinary accounts, node identities and the node handshake quantum-safe from genesis. Web certificates stay classical, and a contract can still implement elliptic-curve or RSA checks itself. Say: "The commitments, proofs, trees, notes, accounts and node transport use only hash-based, lattice-based and symmetric primitives held to at least NIST Category 1; web certificates and cryptography a contract implements itself do not." Also state the effective search cost of the Phantom Key against a quantum attacker, for the 24-word key and for the 12-group key issued before it (C20), and the reveal proof's quantum figures (specification 09, section 4.1).
2. **Revoke, and "has not been revealed", for header-only verifiers (C6, C11, C13, C43).** Block headers carry tree states. The outline does not say they carry the one-time markers. A verifier that checks headers alone can show membership but cannot tell that the commitment has been used up, so "proofs of holding fail after revoke" would not hold for it. Either put the marker set's root in the header, so non-membership of the marker can be proved against a header the verifier already trusts, or require authenticated non-membership evidence bound to the proved commitment and a recent canonical state. A bare answer from a node or endpoint is not enough, because a malicious endpoint (T5) can call a spent commitment unused; if a status provider is trusted instead, that trust is stated as an assumption. The defensible claim is "unrevealed as of height h, on the chain the verifier trusts".
3. **"Reject a second reveal" (C42).** True on one canonical chain. A holder who reveals to a merchant can reveal the same commitment elsewhere on a competing fork. Say: "no marker is used twice on the same chain; a reveal is final to the degree that the chain is."
4. **"No link between commit and reveal" (C3).** Commit shows a public amount and sender. Reveal shows a public recipient and amount. The honest wording is "no cryptographic link; statistical links remain", and the linkage test does not look for them. A second, separate measurement of matching by amount and time against realistic traffic should be run, and the result published; [privacy under attack](research/privacy-under-attack.md) sets out the estimate and the measurement.
5. **"Public accounting is a backstop" (C27).** As written, it is not said whether a reveal that would take the hidden pool below zero is rejected. It should be a consensus rule. Then a proof-system break can drain the pool, but not mint beyond what was burned in, and the damage is bounded and visible.
6. **Installer signature check (C45).** A one-command installer fetched from a host verifies a signature that the same host supplies. That does not protect against a compromised host. The command itself should carry a hash or key fingerprint, so a user can check it against a second source. State that it protects against bad mirrors, not against the first-fetch host.
7. **"Paying with a key" (First product).** Showing the digits gives the recipient full control, but the sender keeps it too until the recipient claims to a new key. It is not payment finality, and the remainder sits in the same position. The app must say so, and Claim must be the step that completes a payment.
8. **Restore and witness fetching (C21, C41).** A wallet that asks an endpoint for one commitment at a time tells the endpoint which commitment is its. Restore must scan the note history in bulk, or the privacy claim does not hold against T5. The specification must say which nodes serve that bulk history (A9).
9. **"First to reveal wins" (C19).** In practice miners decide which of two competing reveals goes first, and the fixed tip means a holder cannot outbid another. Say "the first to be included wins".
10. **"Only the holder can act" (C1).** Add: "Anyone controlling block inclusion can delay or censor a reveal, without being able to redirect or take it."
11. **Proof of holding as a capability (C36).** The version 1 proof of holding (specification 02, statement S3) shows that the prover holds some unrevealed commitment of the type. It names no particular commitment, and the content fingerprint is a field the committer chooses, so anyone can make a commitment of their own and prove holding of it (review 01, F5 and N1). Say: "A proof of holding shows that the prover holds an unrevealed commitment, without showing which. Using it as a capability for a particular resource needs the later layer of granting access." A one-time reveal as a single-use ticket is unaffected.
12. **"A recent, widely shared tree state" (C17).** Since 2026-10-25 the wallet may prove against the root of its own commit's block once that block is 2 deep, when nobody has added a leaf since (specification 02, section 8). The proof is of membership in the whole tree under that root, so it does not single out the leaves of that block cryptographically; it gives a timing hint only. Once a tree is full and the next has started, every leaf of the full tree is proven against its final root, which every holder of a leaf of that tree uses. Say: "A wallet proves against the root of the whole tree as of a block at least 2 deep, never a root that does not hold its leaf; a reveal soon after a lone commit can be matched to it by timing." The size of that tree state is the holder's choice: the wallet may show how many leaves it covers and does not force a wait beyond the 2-block settle depth (specification 02, section 8), so "widely shared" holds only as far as the holder waits for it.

## 7. Attackers outside the model

- **A holder acting against themselves**: losing or leaking the digits.
- **Legal and regulatory action** against the project, miners, or users. The founder skipped legal review knowingly, and this document does not model it.
- **Side channels on the user's own device**: screen capture, malware, a hostile browser extension. They are A7 failures.
- **A break in the proof system, hash or encryption that the whole field misses.** A1 to A3 are assumptions, not claims.

## 9. Majority hashrate at launch

A majority miner (T4) can undo a payment after the recipient has delivered something for it, and spend the same coins or reveal the same commitment again elsewhere. That double spend against exchanges and merchants is the main way small proof-of-work chains have been robbed. Ghost's work function, RandomX, is the one Monero uses, so the hashrate that could attack Ghost already exists, is for rent, and is far larger than Ghost's own network will be. This section estimates the cost, turns it into a confirmation depth by amount, and records the decision on a reorg guard.

Every figure here is an **estimate** from public sources, read on 2026-10-30. Rental prices move daily, and Ghost's launch hashrate is a planning figure until the launch miners are lined up (specification 06, section 4.5). Replace them with measured figures before mainnet.

What the estimate leaves out:

- **Marketplace depth.** Whether NiceHash has enough RandomX orders on offer at those prices at a given moment is not checked here. At the launch figures it needs a tiny fraction of Monero's hashrate, so supply is unlikely to be the limit. For the 630 MH/s row it may be, and an attacker would turn to a pool or botnet instead.
- **Rented or redirected, the result is the same.** A pool such as Qubic, or a botnet, pays no rental price at all; it gives up Monero rewards for the hour, which at Ghost's scale is even less.
- **Block rewards.** The attacker also keeps the rewards of the blocks it mines on the winning chain (120 blocks an hour at about 6.92 GHOST each). The figures above are gross rental cost, so they overstate what the attack costs and understate the depth a recipient needs.

**What past attacks show (public write-ups).**

- **Bitcoin Gold, May 2018:** about 388,000 BTG, around $18 million at the time, double spent against exchanges by a party holding a majority of the hashrate ([BleepingComputer](https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hacker-makes-over-18-million-in-double-spend-attack-on-bitcoin-gold-network)).
- **Ethereum Classic, January 2019:** about $1.1 million double spent through deep reorganisations, reported first by Coinbase ([The Next Web, Hard Fork](https://thenextweb.com/hardfork/2020/01/27/bitcoin-gold-51-percent-attack-blockchain-reorg-cryptocurrency-binance-exchange/)).
- **Bitcoin Gold, January 2020:** two reorganisations up to 16 blocks deep double spent about 7,167 BTG (about $72,000), deep enough to pass an exchange's confirmation window. The rented hashrate was estimated at about $1,200 per attack ([The Next Web, Hard Fork](https://thenextweb.com/hardfork/2020/01/27/bitcoin-gold-51-percent-attack-blockchain-reorg-cryptocurrency-binance-exchange/)).
- **Ethereum Classic, August 2020:** reorganisations of 3,693 blocks (1 August; 807,260 ETC, about $5.6 million, double spent by Bitquery's count, though ETC's developers put it down to a miner's stale software), over 4,000 blocks (6 August, about $1.68 million) and over 7,000 blocks (29 August) ([The Block, 6 August](https://www.theblock.co/news/ecosystems/2020-08-06-ethereum-classic-another-51-attack-74130); [The Block, 31 August](https://www.theblock.co/news/ecosystems/2020-08-31-ethereum-classic-etc-third-51-attack-76431)).

In each case the attack paid because the amount double spent was far larger than the rental, and the recipient waited a fixed number of blocks that did not grow with the amount.

### Confirmation depth by amount

A double spend pays when the amount it can take is larger than what it costs to build a chain with more work than the honest one. The cheapest attacker does not wait for the payment to be buried and then catch up from behind. It mines privately from the payment's parent block, putting the conflicting spend in its own first block, all through the recipient's wait, and releases its chain once the recipient has delivered and its chain has more work. With anything above the honest hashrate it pulls ahead on average during the wait itself, so the extra hashrate buys it nothing: what it must pay for is the work. Whatever hashrate it rents, and however early it starts, its chain must carry more work than the honest chain since the fork point, which is at least the payment's own block and the `z` blocks on top of it. One block is worth `H x 30` hashes, the honest network's hashrate times the block time. The minimum expected rental cost per block of depth, over every hashrate the attacker could choose, is therefore about:

`cost per block = H x p x 30 / 86,400 = H x p / 2,880` dollars

with `H` the network hashrate in kH/s and `p` the NiceHash price in dollars per kH/s per day. An attacker with 1.5 or 2 times the honest hashrate pays this same work bill over a shorter time; one with barely more than the honest hashrate pays it over the whole wait and may run a little longer to pull ahead. Neither does better on average. The depth that makes the attack cost more than it gains is:

`z = V / (cost per block - R)`, rounded up, never fewer than 6 blocks

where:

- `V` is the **total** value that can be double spent in one reorganisation: one reorganisation can undo every payment in its window at once, so an exchange counts all deposits in flight, not one.
- `R` is the market value of one block's reward (about 6.92 GHOST at the start) and the fees in it, which the attacker keeps on its own chain. On the public test network the coins are test coins and `R` is zero. **If `R` is as large as the cost per block, mining is break-even and the attack costs nothing net: no depth makes it unprofitable**, and only the honest miners' share of the hashrate stands between the recipient and a reversal.
- The floor of 6 blocks covers an attacker who does not rent and holds 10% of the mining power: 0.02% chance of overtaking ([privacy under attack](research/privacy-under-attack.md), section 2).

This is an expected cost. An attacker can be lucky: one with less than the honest hashrate succeeds sometimes (the Nakamoto table in [privacy under attack](research/privacy-under-attack.md), section 2), and one with more can finish with less work than average. A recipient who cannot absorb that should wait longer than the table, not shorter.

The table uses the low price of $0.0223 rounded down to $0.02, the cheapest rental seen, and `R` of zero, so that it errs towards waiting longer at launch. It is an **estimate**. Once GHOST has a market price, subtract `R` and the depths grow. Time is at 30 seconds a block.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost per block of depth | $0.0039 | $0.021 | $0.17 | $4.38 |
| $10 | 2,572 blocks (about 21 hours) | 480 blocks (4 hours) | 58 blocks (29 minutes) | 6 blocks (3 minutes) |

Check against the cheaper attack: at 560 kH/s and $0.0223, an attacker mining privately with 1.5 times the honest hashrate through a wait of 2,572 blocks pays about 840 kH/s x $0.0223 x 0.89 days, about $16.70, for an expected lead of about 1,286 blocks. With barely more than the honest hashrate it pays about $11.15, still above the $10 at stake. A depth worked out from catch-up with twice the honest hashrate would be half as deep, 1,286 blocks, and the 1.5 times attacker would undo it for about $8.36.

How to read it:

- **At the expected launch hashrate, no practical depth protects more than a few dollars.** Ten dollars needs nearly a day, and that is before block rewards have any market value. An exchange or merchant that accepts GHOST for something it cannot take back is exposed to anyone who rents an hour of RandomX, whatever depth it picks. Exchanges should not list GHOST, or should cap deposits in flight, until the network's hashrate has grown enough that the table gives a depth they can live with.
- **Use the network's real hashrate, not this table's columns.** A recipient reads `H` from the current difficulty (about difficulty divided by 30 seconds, in hashes a second, then divided by 1,000 for kH/s), takes today's NiceHash price for RandomX and the market value of a block's reward, and applies the formula. Wait for the depth counted from the payment's own block, and count the whole wait as time in which an attacker may already be mining privately from its parent. A sudden drop in hashrate, or a jump in difficulty that nobody expected, is itself a warning.
- **Depth counts from the recipient's own trusted chain.** A verifier who sees only headers, or who may be eclipsed, also needs A9: a private fork (T9) shown only to the victim costs the same per block, so the same depth applies to it.
- **The web app's "Done"** is first inclusion, not settlement ([privacy under attack](research/privacy-under-attack.md), section 2). Anyone receiving value through it applies this table themselves.

### Reorg guard decision

**Decision (2026-10-30): keep plain proof of work at launch, publish the confirmation-depth table above, and revisit a reorg guard (ECBP-1100 or similar) before mainnet.** The guard already in the Core-Geth base stays switched off in Ghost's chain parameters, as decision 0001 and the [Ethereum Classic family survey](surveys/etc-family.md) left it; specification 05, section 7 adds no reorganisation depth limit of its own. Decision 0001 records only standard proof of work for the chosen base, with no finality rule. No decision in `docs/decisions/` rules otherwise. This section records the decision and its trade-offs; it does not design a guard, a finality layer or checkpoints, which the outline keeps out of scope.

**What ECBP-1100 is.** ECBP-1100, "MESS" (Modified Exponential Subjective Scoring), is the rule Ethereum Classic adopted after the 2020 attacks, activated at block 11,380,000. A node running it refuses to switch to a competing chain that forks from its own some time back unless the competing chain has more total work by a factor that grows with the age of the fork point, up to a cap. A deep private fork must then carry many times the honest work, not just a little more. Ethereum Classic later switched it off again with ECIP-1110 ([ECBP index](https://ecips.ethereumclassic.org/ecbp); [ECIP-1110](https://ecips.ethereumclassic.org/ECIPs/ecip-1110); [ETC core developers' ECBP-1100 Q&A](https://ethereumclassic.org/blog/2020-09-23-core-devs-presentation-ecbp-1100-qa)).

**For a guard:**

- It raises the cost of exactly the attack this section prices: a deep reorganisation built in private and released later. For a fork old enough to reach the guard's cap, the depths above would shrink by roughly that factor, because each block of the attack would need that many times the honest work.
- The code is already in the base client and was run on Ethereum Classic mainnet, so it would be switched on, not written.
- It needs no trusted party: no signer, committee or checkpoint authority, so C1 and C46 (no one has power over the chain) still hold.

**Against a guard, compared with plain proof of work:**

- **Fork choice stops being objective.** Plain proof of work (C38) lets any node, at any time, pick the same chain: the one with the most work. Under a guard, two nodes that saw blocks in a different order or at a different time can each refuse the other's chain and stay split until people step in by hand. An attacker can try to cause that split on purpose by releasing a competing chain timed so part of the network sees it first.
- **New and returning nodes see a different rule.** A node that syncs from scratch has no "own chain" to protect and follows most work; a node that was online follows the guard. The web app's header check (A13) follows the chain with the most work from its pinned start, so pages and nodes could disagree about which chain is real.
- **It does little for shallow depths.** The guard's factor starts near one for a young fork and grows with its age. Exchange deposits a few blocks deep, where most small-chain theft has happened, are barely protected, and a majority miner can still censor (T4) without any reorganisation.
- **It is unproven on Ghost.** It was tuned for Ethereum Classic's 13-second blocks and Etchash, not 30-second blocks, RandomX and Ghost's difficulty rule (specification 06), and Ethereum Classic itself removed it. Turning it on is a consensus change that needs its own specification and tests on the test network.

**Compared with the finality layers kept out of scope:** checkpoints or a signed finality layer give hard finality after a set depth, which a guard cannot, but they add a party that decides which chain counts, against "no administrator" (C1) and "maintainers have no power over the chain" (C46). A guard keeps the chain free of such a party and in return gives only soft, local protection. Neither is adopted here.

**Why plain proof of work now.** The network is labelled experimental, the coins on the public test network are test coins, and the honest response to a cheap majority is to say so and to tell recipients how long to wait, which the table does. A guard would not change the launch conclusion (at about a dollar an hour, a few dollars of protection), and it would add split and disagreement risks before Ghost has tested them.

**Before mainnet, revisit with:** the measured launch hashrate and a current NiceHash price in the tables above; how much RandomX hashrate the NiceHash marketplace actually offers; a test-network trial of the guard, including a deliberate split and the web app's chain rule; and whether a different rule fits better. If the decision changes, it goes in `docs/decisions/` and this section is updated.
